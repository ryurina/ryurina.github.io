Understanding the Wiener attack

Why continued fractions break RSA keys with a small private exponent, and where the bound comes from.

14 Aug 2026 · 2 min read
  • RSA
  • Number theory
  • Continued fractions

A small private exponent is tempting: decryption gets cheap. Wiener’s 1990 result says it is also fatal. If the private exponent is small enough relative to the modulus, anyone with the public key can recover it in polynomial time — no factoring required.

This is my attempt to write the argument down in the order it actually makes sense, rather than the order it is usually presented.

The setup

Take an RSA modulus n=pqn = pqn=pq with p<q<2pp < q < 2pp<q<2p, and the usual relation between the public and private exponents:

ed≡1(modφ(n)),φ(n)=(p−1)(q−1).ed \equiv 1 \pmod{\varphi(n)}, \qquad \varphi(n) = (p-1)(q-1).ed≡1(modφ(n)),φ(n)=(p−1)(q−1).

So there is an integer kkk with

ed−kφ(n)=1.ed - k\varphi(n) = 1.ed−kφ(n)=1.

Divide through by dφ(n)d\varphi(n)dφ(n):

eφ(n)−kd=1dφ(n).\frac{e}{\varphi(n)} - \frac{k}{d} = \frac{1}{d\varphi(n)}.φ(n)e​−dk​=dφ(n)1​.

The right-hand side is tiny. That means k/dk/dk/d is a very good rational approximation to e/φ(n)e/\varphi(n)e/φ(n) — and we do not know φ(n)\varphi(n)φ(n), but we do know nnn, and nnn is close to φ(n)\varphi(n)φ(n).

Replacing what we do not know

Since φ(n)=n−p−q+1\varphi(n) = n - p - q + 1φ(n)=n−p−q+1, the gap is

n−φ(n)=p+q−1<3n.n - \varphi(n) = p + q - 1 < 3\sqrt{n}.n−φ(n)=p+q−1<3n​.

Swapping φ(n)\varphi(n)φ(n) for nnn costs us something, but not much. After the estimate you land on the condition that makes the whole thing work:

d<13n1/4.d < \frac{1}{3} n^{1/4}.d<31​n1/4.

Under that bound, k/dk/dk/d satisfies Legendre’s criterion: any rational a/ba/ba/b with

∣x−ab∣<12b2\left| x - \frac{a}{b} \right| < \frac{1}{2b^2}​x−ba​​<2b21​

is a convergent of the continued fraction expansion of xxx.

The attack

That last sentence is the attack. The list of candidates for k/dk/dk/d is not “all fractions” — it is the convergents of e/ne/ne/n, and there are only O(log⁡n)O(\log n)O(logn) of them.

from fractions import Fraction

def convergents(e, n):
    """Convergents of the continued fraction expansion of e/n."""
    frac = Fraction(e, n)
    num_prev, num = 0, 1
    den_prev, den = 1, 0
    while frac.denominator:
        a = frac.numerator // frac.denominator
        num_prev, num = num, a * num + num_prev
        den_prev, den = den, a * den + den_prev
        yield num, den
        frac = Fraction(
            frac.denominator,
            frac.numerator - a * frac.denominator,
        ) if frac.numerator - a * frac.denominator else Fraction(0, 1)
        if frac == 0:
            break

For each candidate pair, test it. A candidate gives a guess

φ=ed−1k,\varphi = \frac{ed - 1}{k},φ=ked−1​,

and then ppp and qqq are the roots of

x2−(n−φ+1)x+n=0.x^2 - (n - \varphi + 1)x + n = 0.x2−(n−φ+1)x+n=0.

If that quadratic has integer roots whose product is nnn, the guess was right and the key is recovered. Otherwise move to the next convergent.

What I take from it

Two things keep striking me about this attack.

The first is that it is not really about RSA. It is a statement about Diophantine approximation that happens to have a key in it. The cryptographic content is the observation that n≈φ(n)n \approx \varphi(n)n≈φ(n).

The second is how narrow the condition is. The bound d<13n1/4d < \tfrac13 n^{1/4}d<31​n1/4 is not where the danger stops — Boneh and Durfee later pushed the vulnerable range to roughly d<n0.292d < n^{0.292}d<n0.292 using lattice methods. “Wiener says I am safe” is not the same as “I am safe”.

Never choose ddd first. Choose eee, and let ddd fall where it falls.
